# SUMMARY

Karl Popper's *The Logic of Scientific Discovery* reframes science as a discipline of bold conjectures exposed to severe tests. Against the idea that science advances by accumulating confirmations, Popper argues that universal theories can never be proven by finite observations but can be refuted by counterinstances. The distinctive mark of a scientific theory is therefore falsifiability: it must take empirical risks by ruling out possible states of affairs.

# IDEAS

- Science cannot be justified by induction, because no number of confirming observations logically proves a universal law.
- A theory is scientific only if it is falsifiable: it must make claims that could conflict with possible observations.
- Confirmations are weak when they are easy to obtain; corroboration matters only when a theory survives risky, severe tests.
- Scientific progress comes from conjectures and refutations, not from passive accumulation of neutral facts.
- Observations are not theory-free; deciding what counts as a basic statement already depends on assumptions, instruments, and conventions.
- Falsification is not mechanical, because scientists can dispute observations, auxiliary assumptions, measurement error, or background conditions.
- Ad hoc modifications that merely protect a theory from refutation reduce its empirical content and scientific value.
- Good theories forbid more; the more a theory rules out, the more informative and testable it becomes.
- Simplicity is valuable because simpler theories usually have greater empirical content and are easier to expose to decisive tests.
- Probability statements create special demarcation challenges because probabilistic theories often cannot be falsified by a single event.
- Theories are never finally verified; even well-corroborated theories remain provisional and open to future rejection.
- The scientist's task is not to seek certainty but to design tests that might reveal error.
- Metaphysical ideas can be meaningful and heuristically useful even when they are not themselves empirically testable.
- Science advances by eliminating error, not by establishing indubitable foundations.
- Rationality in science is critical rationality: willingness to submit claims to public criticism and potential defeat.

# INSIGHTS

- The strength of an explanation is measured less by how many facts it can accommodate than by what evidence would force it to fail.
- A worldview that explains every possible outcome explains too much; unfalsifiability often signals insulation from reality.
- Intellectual honesty requires naming the observations that would change your mind before you start collecting evidence.
- Severe testing turns uncertainty into progress: every failed refutation increases confidence without converting belief into certainty.
- Popper's anti-inductivism is a discipline against confirmation bias, narrative fitting, and after-the-fact rationalization.
- Scientific humility does not mean weak claims; it means bold claims held provisionally and exposed to criticism.
- Falsifiability is a boundary-setting tool, not a complete recipe for actual scientific practice.
- Progress often depends on preserving criticism-friendly institutions as much as on individual genius.
- Theories with high empirical content are useful because they create more opportunities to discover where reality disagrees.
- Corroboration is historical: it records past survival under tests, not a guarantee of future truth.

# TERMS

- **Falsifiability** — A theory's capacity to conflict with possible observations.
- **Demarcation problem** — The problem of distinguishing empirical science from non-science or metaphysics.
- **Problem of induction** — The challenge that finite observations cannot logically justify universal laws.
- **Critical rationalism** — Popper's view that rational inquiry advances through criticism, error correction, and provisional acceptance.
- **Corroboration** — A theory's record of surviving severe tests; not equivalent to verification or proof.
- **Basic statement** — An observation report accepted for the purposes of testing, while still remaining open to criticism.
- **Empirical content** — The range of possible observations a theory rules out; higher content means greater testability.
- **Ad hoc hypothesis** — A protective modification that saves a theory from refutation without increasing testable content.

# HABITS

- Before adopting an explanation, ask: what observation would count against this?
- Prefer theories and plans that make risky, checkable predictions over ones that can explain any outcome.
- Treat confirming evidence cautiously, especially when it was easy to find or expected under many rival explanations.
- Separate a theory's past corroboration from proof; survival so far is not certainty.
- Resist ad hoc excuses that protect a favored view without creating new tests.
- Search deliberately for counterexamples instead of collecting only supporting cases.
- State assumptions and auxiliary conditions when making predictions, so failures cannot be endlessly reinterpreted.
- Reward criticism as a productive tool rather than treating it as disloyalty.
- Keep explanations simple enough to test, compare, and potentially reject.
- Use failed predictions as information, not embarrassment.

# FACTS

- The book was first published in German in 1934 as *Logik der Forschung*.
- The expanded English edition, *The Logic of Scientific Discovery*, appeared in 1959.
- Popper wrote partly against logical positivism's verification principle, replacing verification with falsifiability as the key demarcation criterion.
- The work became foundational for twentieth-century philosophy of science and influenced debates about scientific method, pseudoscience, probability, and rational inquiry.
- Popper's account rejects certainty as the aim of science and replaces it with provisional theories that survive criticism.

# ONE-SENTENCE TAKEAWAY

Science advances not by proving theories true, but by proposing testable conjectures and subjecting them to severe attempts at refutation.

# RECOMMENDATIONS

- Use falsifiability as a practical test for beliefs, strategies, and models: specify what would prove them wrong.
- Judge evidence by severity: confirmations matter most when the theory could easily have failed.
- Beware frameworks that reinterpret every possible outcome as support.
- Build workflows that expose claims to criticism early, before commitment and sunk cost harden around them.
- Treat strong claims as acceptable when they are paired with clear tests and real willingness to revise.
- Preserve a distinction between useful metaphysical inspiration and empirically testable science.
- Read Popper alongside Bayesian reasoning: both reject certainty, but Popper emphasizes criticism and refutation while Bayes emphasizes probabilistic updating.
